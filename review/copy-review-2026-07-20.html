Where the writing reaches for a phrase

Tom,

I have read every page of the site and all four of the public documents on the What the Work Looks Like page, looking for one thing only: places where the writing uses a stock phrase or a borrowed image instead of saying the thing directly.

Two things worth saying before the list. First, the copy is a long way above the standard for this sector. The voice is distinctive, the sentences are short, there is no jargon fog, and the case studies say what actually happened including the parts that went wrong. That is rare and it is the reason the site works. Second, almost everything below is a small edit. None of it is a rewrite.

The list is ranked from most noticeable to least, so if you only change the first ten items you will have taken out most of it.

The two habits underneath the individual phrases

These matter more than any single line, because they repeat.

1. The "not X, it is Y" construction

It appears at least fourteen times across the site and the documents: not an audit, a half-time team talk; not a report telling the client what is wrong; not bureaucracy, it is how good ideas turn into progress; not as an adviser but with the chequebook in his hand; not because everything had always gone right, but because; the problem is not a lack of effort; the purpose is not more paperwork.

One or two of these land well. Fourteen reads as a verbal tic, and it is one of the phrasings readers now most associate with machine-written text. My suggestion is to keep two or three across the whole estate and rewrite the rest as plain statements.

2. "Real", "properly", "actually"

Roughly one sentence in three carries one of these: real businesses, real momentum, real assets, real commercial opportunities, look properly, sold properly, went through everything properly, how it actually operates, who actually delivers.

They are part of the voice, so I would not remove them all. But they tend to sit where a specific fact should be. Built from running real businesses for over 20 years says less than "Twenty years running a taxi firm, a garage and a marine services business" does. Where you can swap one of these words for a detail, the sentence gets stronger.

One thing I have deliberately left alone. The testimonials from Nick, Tristan, Dan and Simon contain a few stock phrases of their own. They are direct quotations, so they should stay exactly as they are. Editing a client's words to sound less ordinary would be worse than the phrase.

A word in defence of the football conceit. It is doing real work. The stadium document is the harder call, because the metaphor is in the title, the cover quote, a page heading and the final line. If you keep it anywhere, keep it on the cover.

If you only do five things

  • Thin the football and stadium metaphors down to the titles and the closing lines. Make every heading in between plain. This is by far the biggest single change and it affects both flagship documents.
  • Replace "Tom Verdict" and "The Tom Rules" with "Recommendation" and "Rules before a project starts". The findings are strong enough that they do not need a byline on every page.
  • Cut the "not X, it is Y" construction from fourteen uses down to two or three.
  • Change the hero: "constant firefighting" and "Tell us about your fires" are the first two things a visitor reads, and they are the most worn phrases on the site. "Tell us what is going on" is already your best line. Use it in both places.
  • Swap "seven figures" and "real businesses" for actual numbers and actual names. Specifics are more persuasive than emphasis, and you have the specifics.

None of this is urgent and none of it is doing you damage as it stands. It is the difference between copy that is clearly better than the competition and copy that sounds like nobody else could have written it.

Happy to make any of these changes directly, or to leave the list with you and change nothing.
