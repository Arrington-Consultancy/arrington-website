How to Stop Being the Bottleneck in Your Owner Run Business
18 November 2026

I spent years being the busiest person in the building and calling it hard work.

The business was growing. Customers were looked after. Wages were paid. And I was across almost everything. That sounds like success, and in many ways it was. But it also meant that every decision, every problem and every small question came back to me. I was the bottleneck, and I didn't really notice because I was good at the job.

If you run an established owner run business in Devon, Cornwall or the wider South West, you may recognise this. You might have strong revenue, loyal customers and capable people. Yet too much still depends on you. The question is not whether the business is doing well. The question is whether it could carry on sensibly without you in every conversation.

Why it happens

Owners are usually the best person for the job when the business starts. You know the customers, the margins and the people. So problems get routed to you, and you solve them. That works until the volume grows faster than your time.

The uncomfortable part is that the habit often comes from the owner. We answer the phone, approve the invoice and make the call because it feels faster. It usually is faster in the moment. Over a year, it becomes the way the business runs.

Where the time actually goes

When we work with owners, we usually look at three places first.

Decisions only you can make. Some decisions really do need the owner, such as the big strategic calls and the ones that carry personal risk. But many routine decisions do not. Write down the last ten decisions you made in a normal week. Be honest about how many of them someone else could have made with clear guidance.

Work you repeat every week. Ordering, chasing, checking, reconciling and rebooking are often repeated for years. Each one is small. Together they can take a surprising share of your week.

Costs you have not reviewed in a while. This is where the owner's time is most expensive. I once discovered that a telephone contract I had signed was costing around £850 a month more than a replacement would have. The bill had jumped to around £1,200 a month. By then the business had grown and work I had once carried myself was spread across other people, which gave me the time to fight it. The difference was just over £10,000 a year.

The lesson was not that I should have been better at contracts. It was that I had no time to look.

Rules and fixes that backfire

When the owner is stretched, the natural response is another rule. Rules can help. But they also change behaviour, often in ways nobody planned.

In one of our taxi businesses, a first in, first out system for longer journeys made drivers start earlier and finish earlier. A minimum shift fixed one problem and created another. Every change was sensible on its own. The combined result was still wrong until we looked at the whole system.

The same applies to your business. Before you add a rule to fix a problem, ask what behaviour it will reward, and who will be affected.

Where to start this month

You do not need a full restructure to make progress. Start small.

Write down the three decisions you make most often that someone else could make with clear guidance.
Pick one of them and decide who will own it from next week.
Write a short note explaining what good looks like, so they do not need to ask you every time.
Review it in four weeks. Remove yourself from one more task.

This is slow work. It is also the work that makes a business easier to run and easier to sell, if that is ever your plan.
